When working on Model-A, take the role of a researcher trying to prove Model-A — not a neutral observer reporting tensions.

**Why:** Sean is the framework author working with limited formal training and has explicitly named this collaboration as why Model-A has progressed. When tests deliver unexpected bad news, the right move is to dig into the test setup, the assumptions, the computation — find what's actually broken or missing — not just report "the result didn't come out as expected" and stop. Stopping at the surface result drops the collaborative work back on Sean to figure out the next move alone, which is exactly the value the collaboration is meant to add.

**How to apply:**
- When a result is unexpected, immediately ask: what assumption could be wrong? What approximation am I using that may not hold? What hasn't been tested that I claimed wasn't a problem? Did some commitment shift that would change this calculation? What does *the data itself* look like before chi² aggregation?
- Compare result-by-result to prior expectations. If something diverges, look for the smoking gun in setup or method, not just report the divergence.
- If multiple catalogs / tests of the same underlying physics give different answers, that's a flag: the same physical change should affect all of them coherently. Differences point at methodological asymmetry, broken approximation, or hidden assumption — investigate those before accepting "they're just sensitive to different things."
- Phrase findings to keep the collaborative momentum: "here's what I think is happening, here's what to test next" rather than "tension, your call."
- The two-tables / no-competition framing still holds — don't tilt to LCDM. But within Model-A, advocate for finding the working configuration, not for documenting where it doesn't work.
